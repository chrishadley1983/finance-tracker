The plan in three moves

  1. Buy the retirement directly. A ladder of index-linked gilts, one maturing each year 2035–2045, pays us £60k (in today's money) every year no matter what markets do. Cost: ~£498k. Full explanation on the next page.
  2. Let the rest grow. ~£1M stays in global equities. Its only required job is the years after the ladder ends, net of two state pensions — a very low bar.
  3. Ratchet. Once a year, if the equity pot has grown well past what the plan still needs from it (~25% ahead of target), sell the excess and buy more certainty: top up the cash buffer, then the spare 2041 rung, then rungs for earlier years if we fancy retiring sooner. It only works one way — we never sell gilts to buy shares after a crash.

The gilt ladder, in one page

An index-linked gilt is a loan to the UK government that repays with inflation added: buy one maturing in 2037 and, come 2037, back comes the money in 2037 purchasing power, plus interest along the way. The ladder is eleven of them, one maturing each year from 2035 to 2045, each sized to hand us £60k in today's money in its year.

Holding to maturity is the whole trick. Gilt prices wobble daily, but a rung held to the end pays exactly what it promised, with HM Government on the other side. This is the retirement bought outright, rather than a bet that markets deliver it. And the window to build it is open now: after-inflation yields at these maturities are 1.6–2.3% — five years ago they were negative and this ladder couldn't be built at any sensible price.

Why that wrapper split, and not more in the ISA? Because ISA money is the only money that can fund 2035–40 — both pensions are locked until Chris turns 57 in Nov 2040. So the scarce ISA space goes only where nothing else works, and the moment the SIPP opens, its rungs take over. That's the more tax-efficient order, not less: the SIPP rungs were bought with pre-tax pounds (40% relief on the way in) and come out against our personal allowances at almost no tax.

What can go wrong: the government not paying (remoter than the bank failing); needing to sell a rung early, which happens at that day's price rather than the promised one — fine, just not guaranteed. Inflation is handled by construction. That's the list.

The timeline

Both of us stop work in June 2035 (Max finishes Year 11) — Chris 51, Abby 48. HB carries on at ~£13k/yr until Chris's pension opens in Nov 2040: the profits sit inside his personal allowance so carry no tax, his NI stamp comes free (profits above £7,105 earn the year automatically), and the rest is holiday pocket money. It stops once pension withdrawals want that allowance instead. We spend £60k/yr. Pensions unlock at 57 (2040 / 2043); state pensions at 68 (2051 / 2054, £12.5k each).

How the trap works

Above £60,000 of income (tested per person on the higher earner, so Chris's HB income is irrelevant), child benefit is clawed back 1% per £200, gone at £80,000. Pension contributions don't count as income — so enough AVC puts Abby back under the line, and the tax system pays 42p of every sacrificed pound:

At stake to Aug 2035 (Emmie) and Aug 2037 (Max — his last two years come free once Abby's retired anyway). Between £60–80k each extra £1 earned loses ~54p all-in.

We'd aim at £59,500, leaving a £500 buffer against payroll noise. Peak pension input is £42.8k against the £60k annual allowance, so there's room throughout.

And if we brought retirement forward? Because markets have been kind, or redundancy, or illness in the family, or we simply feel ready — and assuming HB stops at the same time. The price of each year earlier is roughly one year's spending from the taxable side (£65–70k) plus that year's pension contributions forgone — and because that money then misses forty years of growth, it shows up as ~£250–270k less at 92 per year brought forward.

So even four years early, the sustainable spend stays £20k+ clear of what we've planned. The extra risk is practical rather than financial: the bridge before Chris's pension stretches (five years becomes nine, so more rungs must come from the ISA side, thinning the liquid margin), more pre-pension years sit exposed to any rule change, and a few more NI stamp years need covering. All manageable — which is the point.

The mechanics are the ratchet pointed backwards: buy the missing early rungs (2034, 2033…) out of the growth pot, exactly as we'd have bought the later ones. Two things soften it further: with salaries gone, income drops under £60k, so full child benefit returns with no sacrifice needed for as long as the kids qualify; and if the trigger is redundancy, the payoff funds a good chunk of the extra years by itself. Early exit costs a few NI stamp years (fixable for a few hundred pounds each) and shrinks the surplus — which also shrinks the inheritance-tax problem we'd otherwise be managing (pensions join the estate from April 2027; drawing at 15% in our 70s beats leaving 40% to HMRC — a conversation for the someday list). The honest constraint on going early isn't money; it's whether we're ready.
